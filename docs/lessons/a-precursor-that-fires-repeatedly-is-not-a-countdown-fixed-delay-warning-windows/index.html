A precursor that fires repeatedly is not a countdown: fixed-delay warning windows miss the failure

Problem

A crashing service emitted a precursor signal before failing. From one or two observations, the gap was estimated as a fixed delay — "the trigger signature appears 30 seconds before the crash" — and that number was designed into the alerting: arm a warning window N seconds before the expected failure.

The number then propagated. It appeared as settled fact in several write-ups ("触发签名早于崩溃 30 秒"), each one building on the estimate rather than on a measurement.

Continuing to sample broke it. The precursor is not a one-shot event:

  • the same precursor fires repeatedly inside a single failure cycle — the count goes 1, 2, 3…;
  • the failure can arrive after any of those occurrences;
  • the observed precursor→failure gap ranged from 15 seconds to several minutes.

A fixed window therefore fails in both directions:

  • too short (e.g. 30 s): the first precursor arrives, the window opens and closes, the alert is dismissed — and the failure comes minutes later. Net warning: none, but the system reported having warned.
  • too long: every occurrence of a repeated precursor holds the alert open for a long time. The signal is nearly always on, users learn to ignore it, and it is muted exactly when it matters.

Root Cause

"The precursor appeared" was read as "the countdown started". It is not a countdown. It is closer to the trigger conditions are accumulating: each occurrence means one more unit of accumulation has happened, not that a timer began at that instant.

Inferring a fixed delay from a single precursor→failure gap treats the last accumulation as the only one:

observed:  [precursor] ............ 30 s ............ [failure]
model:     fixed 30 s delay
reality:   [p] ... [p] ........ [p] ...... [p] ...... [failure]
           ^ first                   ^ last  \_ the 30 s that got measured

The 30 seconds was the tail of an accumulation sequence. With one or two samples there is nothing to distinguish the tail from the whole.

The compounding error is social, not statistical: once "30 seconds" is written into a report as fact, later observations get reconciled against it rather than replacing it, so a sampling artifact survives into the design. The next reader has no way to tell an estimate from a measurement.

Solution

Design the early warning on accumulation count and rate, not on a fixed delay window.

1. Record precursors as count events, not as moments

Keep every occurrence's timestamp. When the failure happens, compute three quantities per cycle:

precursor_count        = number of occurrences in the cycle
first_to_failure       = t(failure) - t(first precursor)   ← lead time available
last_to_failure        = t(failure) - t(last precursor)    ← response time available

Two different questions, two different numbers. Conflating them is how the fixed delay was invented: the second quantity is short and stable; the first is long and variable.

2. Alert state is "unresolved", not "timed"

After a precursor appears, hold the alert until one of two things happens:

  • the failure occurs (the alert was right), or
  • an explicit healthy signal arrives — e.g. the log keeps growing with no new precursor for a defined period, or the service reports a successful operation.

Do not auto-disarm on a fixed number of seconds. A timer encodes an assumption about when the failure comes; "unresolved" only encodes that nothing has resolved it yet, which is true whether the failure lands in 15 seconds or four minutes.

3. Report both numbers with their labels

  • "末次前兆→故障" — usable as response time for emergency handling.
  • "首次前兆→故障" — usable as early-warning lead time for design.

Any sentence of the form "the precursor is N seconds before the failure" is incomplete. It must say which precursor: "the last precursor was 30 seconds before the failure" and "the first precursor was up to several minutes before it". Use the second figure when sizing a warning window, and the first when sizing an on-call response.

Classifying what you have

observationwhat it meansdesign consequence
precursor appears once, failure exactly N seconds later, repeatablytrue fixed delaya fixed window is legitimate
precursor appears multiple times, failure after any onecumulativeunresolved alert + cumulative count
precursor appears, failure does not follow this timeprecursor is not a sufficient conditionthe alert must be releasable, or it becomes a permanent false alarm

The first row is the one people assume. The report's data lands in the second, which is why the fixed window under-warned.

Verification

Collect several complete failure cycles (not one) and tabulate per cycle:

# t_precursors: list of timestamps, one cycle; t_failure: failure timestamp
count            = len(t_precursors)
first_to_failure = (t_failure - t_precursors[0]).total_seconds()
last_to_failure  = (t_failure - t_precursors[-1]).total_seconds()

Then apply the decision rule:

  • count > 1 in most cycles → cumulative, not fixed delay;
  • the first_to_failure distribution is clearly wider than the last_to_failure distribution → the fixed-window design is wrong, switch to unresolved-alert + cumulative count;
  • last_to_failure is short and tight while first_to_failure is long and wide → this is the exact profile that produces a believable "N seconds" estimate, and the exact profile a fixed window cannot cover. It should be treated as evidence against the fixed window, not for it.

Also verify the write-up: each claim of the form "N seconds before the failure" carries the qualifier identifying which precursor it refers to. A report without that qualifier fails verification even if the number itself is correct.

Notes

  • Evidence level E1: one operator's account, with the precursor counts and the 15 s … several-minutes spread as reported; not reproduced in this repository. The specific "30 seconds" was the estimate in the reports, not a measured constant.
  • The sample-size trap is the transferable part. A single precursor→failure pair is indistinguishable from a fixed delay. The estimator cannot know it picked the last item of a sequence until a second sequence is observed. When a "fixed N seconds" claim is about to enter a design, the check is cheap: observe a second full cycle before building on it.
  • A precursor that sometimes does not lead to a failure means the signal is necessary but not sufficient. Any design that disarms only on failure therefore needs a health-based release, or it fires forever after the first non-followed precursor.
  • The false-alarm failure mode is asymmetric in cost: an over-long window does not just annoy, it trains people to ignore the signal, so its cost is paid at the one time it was right.
  • Related in this corpus: a watchdog keyed on the increment of an error signature stops firing once the target has already crashed (watchdog-incremental-only-misses-crashed-frozen-target) — the same family of "the alert condition assumed the failure starts when I am watching".
